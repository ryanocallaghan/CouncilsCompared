About the figures

Councils compared puts official statistics about councils side by side, so you can see how yours does compared with others.

Which councils are included

The {{ councils | length }} councils in England that run all local services: unitary authorities, metropolitan districts and London boroughs. In areas with a county council and district councils, services are split between them, so they are not included. The City of London and the Isles of Scilly are left out because they are very small and share many services.

{% if "Scotland" in model.nations() %}

All 32 councils in Scotland are included too. Councils in Scotland run different services from councils in England, and the statistics are collected differently, so Scottish councils are only compared with each other. Most of their figures come from the Local Government Benchmarking Framework, which every Scottish council reports to. Complaints come from the Scottish Public Services Ombudsman, and homelessness, spending and council tax from the Scottish Government. In Scotland all of the council tax goes to the council, and water and sewerage charges are added to the bill for Scottish Water.

{% endif %}

How councils are compared

For each measure, councils are put in order from best to worst, and the position shows where a council comes in the group. Councils with the same figure share a position. A council in the bottom quarter is shown in red, below the middle in yellow, and in the top half in green.

You can compare a council with:

  • every council included, across England
  • councils in the same region
  • councils of the same type, such as other metropolitan districts
  • similar councils: the Department for Education's statistical neighbours, the councils most like it on things that drive demand for services such as deprivation and population (county councils are left out of these lists)
{% if "Scotland" in model.nations() %}

A Scottish council is compared with every council in Scotland, and with the other councils in its family group: the Local Government Benchmarking Framework puts councils in groups of eight by how deprived they are.

{% endif %}

Rates per resident use the ONS population estimate for {{ sources.population.period }} for every year.

What to bear in mind

  • Councils serve very different places. A low position can reflect more demand, less money or different local choices, not only how well a council is run.
  • Ombudsman figures by service area count the decisions the Ombudsman publishes, which cover most but not all upheld complaints. Shares of upheld cases are only shown for councils with at least 5 cases.
  • Fly-tipping figures depend on how each council records incidents.
  • Council tax and business rates collection is the share collected in the year it was due. Councils keep collecting after that.
  • Homelessness figures are for a single quarter. Shares are only shown where at least 10 cases ended. Where very small numbers of families in temporary accommodation are suppressed, they are counted as none. Families kept in bed and breakfast accommodation for more than 6 weeks are shown on each council's page but not ranked.
  • Councils that no longer own council homes have no council housing figures.
  • The website score is an automated accessibility test. It finds many problems, but not all.
    • {% if "Scotland" in model.nations() %}
  • In Scotland, satisfaction with bin collections and street cleaning comes from the Scottish Household Survey. The samples in each council are small, so the figures are averages over three years.
  • Scottish figures cover different years: each measure shows the year its figures are for.
    • {% endif %}

Where the figures come from

The figures are updated automatically: every week, a script checks each publication for a new edition and proposes the changes, which are checked before they go live. The code is public.

Who runs this website

I'm Ryan O'Callaghan, and Councils compared is my personal project. It's independent: it's not run by, funded by or connected to GOV.UK, the government, any council, my employer, or any of the organisations whose figures it uses.

Every figure comes from a published source and the code is public, so you can check how it was put together.

If a figure looks wrong, or you have an idea, get in touch or report it on GitHub.
